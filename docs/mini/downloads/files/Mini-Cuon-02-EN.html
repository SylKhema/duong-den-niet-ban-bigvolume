ATTENTION TO REPULSIVENESS AND THE SKELETON MEDITATION SUBJECT In this second part of tranquillity meditation, I shall continue by briefly explaining how to develop the four protective meditation subjects before proceeding to insight. For most meditators, developing those protective subjects is easier after developing the white kasiṇa, or the ten kasiṇas and eight attainments. This also greatly supports the detailed method of four-elements meditation. Since there are also many methods of discerning the meditation subject of the four elements, I shall first describe how to discern the thirty-two parts of the body, the skeleton meditation subject, and how to proceed from the skeleton to the white kasiṇa and then, successively, through the eight attainments on each kasiṇa.

Re-establish ānāpāna fourth-jhāna concentration until the light of wisdom shines brilliantly. When its light is very strong, use it to discern the thirty-two parts of the body. Divide the twenty body parts with predominant earth element into four groups of five, and the twelve with predominant water element into two groups of six. Begin with one group at a time. When, looking in a clear mirror, you can see your own face distinctly, you should be able to see the thirty-two parts just as distinctly. If the light of knowledge fades and the body parts become unclear while discerning them, re-establish ānāpāna fourth-jhāna concentration…

ATTENTION TO REPULSIVENESS (PAṬIKŪLAMANASIKĀRA)

Ettha pana attano jīvamānakasarīre paṭikūlanti parikammaṃ karontassa appanāpi upacārampi jāyati. Parassa jīvamānakasarīre paṭikūlanti manasikarontassa neva appanā jāyati, na upacāraṃ. Nanu ca dasasu asubhesu ubhayampetaṃ jāyatīti. Āma jāyati. Tāni hi anupādinnakapakkhe ṭhitāni, tasmā tattha appanāpi upacārampi jāyati. Idaṃ pana upādinnakapakkhe ṭhitaṃ, tenevettha ubhayampetaṃ na jāyati. Asubhānupassanāsaṅkhātā pana vipassanābhāvanā hotīti veditabbā. (Abhi-ṭṭha, 2, 248.)

Here, when carrying out the preliminary work on one's own living body as repulsive, both access and absorption arise. When attending to another living body as repulsive, neither absorption nor access arises. But does not the Asubhasutta in the Suttanipāta teach that access and absorption arise in both cases? That discourse concerns a body without consciousness. In that case both occur. What is intended here, however, is hair, nails, and so forth in a living body. Attention to repulsiveness in them brings neither access nor absorption, but is beneficial to the perception of foulness. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, p. 243.)

Internal and external repulsiveness

According to this commentary, when the meditator carries out preliminary work on one or several of the thirty-two parts of his own living body as “repulsive, repulsive,” either access or absorption jhāna can occur. When he carries out preliminary work on the body parts of another living being, external to himself, as “repulsive, repulsive,” neither access nor absorption occurs. Puna caparaṃ, bhikkhave, bhikkhu seyyathāpi passeyya sarīraṃ sivathikāya chaḍḍitaṃ ekāhamataṃ vā dvīhamataṃ vā tīhamataṃ vā uddhumātakaṃ vinīlakaṃ vipubbakajātaṃ. So imameva kāyaṃ upasaṃharati: “Ayampi kho kāyo evaṃdhammo evaṃbhāvī evaṃanatīto”ti. (Ma 1,73.)

Again, bhikkhus, as though seeing a corpse thrown into a charnel ground, one day, two days, or three days dead, swollen, discoloured, and festering, a bhikkhu compares this very body with it: “This body too is of that nature, will become like that, and has not gone beyond that.”

Tasmā tattha hatamatādisatte oloketvā diṭṭhapubbasampattīnaṃ sattānaṃ matānaṃ maraṇaṃ āvajjetvā satiñca saṃvegañca ñāṇañca yojetvā “maraṇaṃ bhavissatī”tiādinā nayena manasikāro pavattetabbo. (Visuddhi 1,223.)

Therefore, look at beings who have been killed or have died here and there, and bring to mind the death of beings who died amid prosperity. With mindfulness, a sense of urgency, and discernment, attend in such ways as “Death will come.”

Following these passages, to develop mindfulness of death, first re-establish concentration on foulness up to the first jhāna. When you can contemplate the repulsive corpse up to first-jhāna concentration, direct discernment to your own death: “This body of mine too is subject to such death. This death will surely occur. I cannot go beyond it.” Looking with mindfulness, urgency, and discernment, you will see your own repulsive corpse. Then use discernment to see the cutting off of the life faculty in that image of your own death…

The cutting off of the life faculty

This is a difficult contemplation because it concerns an ultimate reality, the life faculty. The Visuddhimagga defines mindfulness of death as recollecting death as the cutting off of the life faculty. Practise so as to see the cutting off of your own life faculty. Merely bringing to mind and seeing your own corpse with discernment does not complete the task. Therefore, once the meditator…

Discerning the cessation of the life faculty

…has developed first-jhāna concentration with a repulsive corpse as object, they can direct discernment towards the image of their own death. When you see your corpse, direct attention inside its heart. You may not immediately see the cutting off of the life faculty, since in that corpse the faculty has already been cut off.

When you can direct discernment into the heart in this way, look back a little to the time before death. You may then see, near the time of death, the clarity of the life-continuum mind-door or the stream of consciousness. Here the intended object is the clarity of the life-continuum mind-door. Since you have already discerned it when discerning the jhāna factors, you can discern it readily here too. When you see it with discernment, move closer to the time of death. For most meditators who have never discerned mentality, however, at this stage it is very difficult to understand the life-continuum consciousness and the life faculty.

The four protective meditation subjects

Even if this is difficult, you have already seen the clarity of the life-continuum mind-door and can develop mindfulness of death by taking its cessation as object. As explained above, this clarity is called the life-continuum by way of a figurative designation. With discernment you can still bring your own corpse into view. Someone who has practised mindfulness of death in past lives may see the cutting off of the life faculty, or, put another way, the cessation of the life-continuum consciousness together with the life faculty. It is best if this can be seen.

Most meditators who have yet to discern mentality can see the cessation of the clarity of the life-continuum mind-door just explained, called the life-continuum figuratively. They can develop mindfulness of death by taking as object the cessation of that material clarity. Meditators who have discerned the relations of cause and effect in dependent origination up to knowledge of discerning conditions can readily see the cessation of the life-continuum consciousness together with the life faculty.

Loving-kindness, recollection of the Buddha, foulness, and mindfulness of death are the four protective meditation subjects. They are called protective because they guard meditators. They are subjects to learn and cultivate before proceeding to insight.

Choosing a subject to counter a hindrance

Miến / Burmese 135

Asubhā bhāvetabbā rāgassa pahānāya. Mettā bhāvetabbā byāpādassa pahānāya. Ānāpānassati bhāvetabbā vitakkupacchedāya. (Aṅ 3,169; Udāna 120, Meghiyasutta.)

Develop foulness for abandoning lust. Develop loving-kindness for abandoning ill will. Develop mindfulness of breathing for cutting off applied thoughts.

According to this teaching in the Meghiya Sutta, foulness is an excellent means of countering lust. The foulness meditation described above, with a corpse as its object, is foulness connected with the lifeless. The Girimānanda Sutta teaches another subject of foulness, attending to the thirty-two parts of a living body as repulsive. This is foulness connected with the living. Both kinds are excellent means of removing lust. Loving-kindness is an excellent subject for removing ill will and aversion. Mindfulness of breathing is an effective subject for countering plans and other kinds of thought.

Therefore, good meditator, develop foulness when lust is strong, loving-kindness when aversion is strong, and mindfulness of breathing when thoughts proliferate. When faith diminishes and the mind lacks clarity in meditation, develop recollection of the Buddha. When urgency diminishes and you become lax in meditation, develop mindfulness of death.

Conclusion of the section on tranquillity meditation

The Buddha taught forty meditation subjects for tranquillity. Of these, this section has presented the following subjects in brief.

1. Mindfulness of breathing. 2. Attention to repulsiveness, the meditation on the bones. 3. The ten kasiṇas and the eight attainments. 4. The four divine abidings. 5. Recollection of the Buddha. 6. Meditation on foulness. 7. Mindfulness of death.

Anyone wishing to cultivate the remaining tranquillity subjects should approach teachers and learn from them. If you wish to proceed to insight meditation, continue by developing the subjects beginning with the discernment of materiality.
