The teaching of dependent origination

The Buddha did not establish or create this principle of dependent origination. Whether or not Buddhas arise, the principle still holds. Only when Buddhas arise and disclose and teach it do beings have the opportunity to know it. If a Buddha does not arise, if people do not hear the teaching when one arises, or if, even after hearing it, they cling to such wrong views as eternalism and annihilationism, they cannot understand this principle of dependent origination.

Dependent origination is deep and appears deep, difficult and appears difficult. When Venerable Ānanda said that it was easy and shallow, the Buddha restrained him: “Mā hevaṃ, Ānanda … Ānanda, do not say so.” However difficult and profound this teaching may be, one who has yet to know dependent origination clearly cannot escape the suffering of saṃsāra. One who has yet to know this teaching does not deserve to be called an ascetic or a brahmin.

The Buddhas of the past, present, and future attain Buddhahood through clearly knowing dependent origination. As long as beings have yet to know it clearly, they continue to fall into one of two abysses, eternalism and annihilationism, and undergo all kinds of suffering. Therefore, in the Nidānasaṃyutta the Buddha taught that anyone wishing to escape the suffering of saṃsāra must strive to penetrate this teaching of dependent origination. (From the nissaya of the Nidānavaggasaṃyutta.)

Part 1. The declaration in the Mahānidāna Sutta

Part 1. The declaration in the Great Discourse on Causation (Mahānidānasutta).

At that time the Buddha was staying in the country of the Kurus, relying on the town of Kammāsadhamma for alms. On the day the Buddha was to give the Great Discourse on Causation, Venerable Ānanda went to his daytime retreat, sat down, and entered the fruition attainment of stream-entry. When the time he had determined elapsed, he emerged from that attainment and directed his mind to dependent origination, also called the pattern of conditions (paccayākāra). Venerable Ānanda discerned it in the following ways:

1. From ignorance to aging and death, in the forward order of dependent origination (anuloma).
2. From aging and death to ignorance, in the reverse order of dependent origination (paṭiloma).



Four ways of discerning dependent origination and Ānanda’s statement
Miến / Burmese 2

3. From both ends toward the middle, in the forward and reverse orders of dependent origination.
4. From the middle toward both ends, in the forward and reverse orders of dependent origination.

He developed insight into each of the twelve factors of dependent origination in turn, using all four methods, as one might trace lines on the ground. As Venerable Ānanda developed insight in this way, the relation between cause and effect in dependent origination, also called the pattern of conditions (paccayākāra), became clear to his knowledge.

He thought that if he presented this to the Buddha, he might hear a fine discourse. That evening he went to the Buddha, paid homage, sat to one side, and said:

“It is wonderful, venerable sir! It is astonishing, venerable sir! This dependent origination is very deep and appears deep, yet to me it seems quite plain, venerable sir.”

The Buddha then taught the Great Discourse on Causation to Venerable Ānanda. The following passage is an excerpt from that discourse.



The depth of dependent origination and the three kinds of full understanding
Miến / Burmese 3

Mā hevaṃ, Ānanda, avaca, mā hevaṃ, Ānanda, avaca. Gambhīro cāyaṃ, Ānanda, paṭiccasamuppādo gambhīrāvabhāso ca. Etassa, Ānanda, dhammassa ananubodhā appaṭivedhā evamayaṃ pajā tantākulakajātā kulagaṇṭhikajātā muñjapabbajabhūtā apāyaṃ duggatiṃ vinipātaṃ saṃsāraṃ nātivattati. (Dīgha, vol. 2, p. 47.)

Ananubodhāti ñātapariññāvasena ananubujjhanā. Appaṭivedhāti tīraṇapahānapariññāvasena appaṭivijjhanā. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 85.)

“Ānanda, do not say so. Ānanda, do not say so. This relation of cause and effect, the teaching of dependent origination, is deep. Because it is deep, it also appears deep.”

“Ānanda, because beings have not understood and penetrated that relation of cause and effect by the three kinds of full understanding—full understanding as knowing (ñātapariññā), as investigation (tīraṇapariññā), and as abandoning (pahānapariññā)—in this long round of rebirths they are tangled like a skein of thread, knotted like a bundle of fibres, and matted like muñja and pabbaja grasses. They cannot pass beyond the round of rebirths in the realms of misery, bad destinations, and states of downfall, where wrongdoers go, deprived of well-being and subject to ruin. They cannot pass beyond the round of rebirths in the good destinations outside those realms of misery either.” (Dīgha, vol. 2, p. 47.)

Drawing on the teaching above, the commentators then make the following determination.



Breaking the wheel of becoming with the sword of knowledge
Miến / Burmese 4

Ñāṇāsinā samādhipavarasilāya sunisitena —
Bhavacakkamapadāletvā, asanivicakkamiva niccanimmathaṃ.
Saṃsārabhayamatīto, na koci supinantarepyatthi.
(Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 2, p. 189. Visuddhimagga, vol. 2, p. 221.)

The sword of knowledge is honed on the excellent whetstone of concentration. The wheel of becoming, the causal connection of dependent origination within saṃsāra, continually grinds down and oppresses beings like a wheel of thunderbolts. Without breaking that wheel with this sword, no one has ever passed beyond the great danger of suffering in saṃsāra, even in a dream. (Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 2, p. 189. Visuddhimagga, vol. 2, p. 221.)

In accordance with the Pāli and the commentary above, a good person who seeks the true Dhamma and aspires to the sublime peace of Nibbāna must strive to know the causal connection of dependent origination clearly through three kinds of full understanding: full understanding as knowing (ñātapariññā), called understanding (anubodha), and full understanding as investigation (tīraṇapariññā) and as abandoning (pahānapariññā), called penetration (paṭivedha). The Buddha himself taught that beings have yet to pass beyond saṃsāra because they have not repeatedly known it through understanding and have not personally penetrated it through to the moments of path and fruition. Those who wish to escape saṃsāra must therefore strive for deep knowledge through both understanding and penetration. This is a task that calls for effort to know it for oneself. As for feeling one’s way guided solely by a teacher’s words…

…understand that this task cannot be accomplished in that way.

What is a stream-enterer?

The causal connection of dependent origination is deep and appears deep, difficult and appears difficult. Why, then, did it appear to Venerable Ānanda’s knowledge to be “easy to understand, as though shallow”? The answer is as follows.

Therassa hi catūhi kāraṇehi gambhīropi paṭiccasamuppādo uttānoti upaṭṭhāsi. Katamehi catūhi? Pubbūpanissayasampattiyā, titthavāsena, sotāpannatāya, bahussutabhāvenāti. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 78.)

For the following four reasons, the causal connection of dependent origination, though deep and difficult, appeared clear to Venerable Ānanda’s knowledge:
1. Pubbūpanissayasampatti — possessing the perfections cultivated in former lives.
2. Titthavāsa — continually living near the good fords in the dispensation.
3. Sotāpannatā — having become a stream-enterer.
4. Bahussutabhāva — being learned in the teachings.

It was owing to these four conditions that the causal connection of dependent origination, deep and difficult as it is…



Former supporting conditions and living near a good ford
Miến / Burmese 6

…appeared clearly to Venerable Ānanda’s knowledge.

1. Pubbūpanissayasampatti. In the dispensation of Buddha Padumuttara, Venerable Ānanda made an aspiration and cultivated the perfections for a hundred thousand aeons in order to attain foremost rank in five respects. All the perfections accumulated through many lives for the great disciple’s awakening that would enable him to hold these five foremost ranks were now fulfilled in his continuity. This is one condition.

2. Titthavāsa. Good fords on the way to Nibbāna are called tittha. The Perfectly Awakened Buddha and his foremost great disciples can correctly show the way to Nibbāna within the dispensation. They are called tittha because they are like good fords for crossing to the farther shore, Nibbāna. Living near and relying on such teachers is called titthavāsa. Venerable Ānanda possessed this quality too.

Whether with the Buddha or with foremost great disciples such as Venerable Sāriputta, Mahāmoggallāna, Mahākoṭṭhita, and Mahāpanthaka, Venerable Ānanda practised the following in the presence of those eminent teachers:
(a) Uggaha — learning the Pāli text.
(b) Savana — listening to its meaning, that is, to the commentary (atthakathā or aṭṭhakathā).



Learning from teachers and attaining stream-entry
Miến / Burmese 7

(c) Paripucchā — asking further about the meaning and commentary on profound and difficult points.
(d) Dhāraṇā — retaining both the Pāli text and its meaning in the commentary, keeping them in memory.

In this way he systematically learned the Pāli and commentaries on dependent origination and retained them in memory. For this reason too, dependent origination, though deep and difficult, appeared plainly to Venerable Ānanda’s knowledge as something easy to understand. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 83.)

3. Sotāpannatā — having become a stream-enterer.

Sotāpannānañca nāma paccayākāro uttānakova hutvā upaṭṭhāti. Ayañca āyasmā sotāpanno. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 83.)

Sotāpannānañca … pe … upaṭṭhāti tattha sammohaviddhaṃsanena. “Yaṃ kiñci samudayadhammaṃ, sabbaṃ taṃ nirodhadhamman”ti attapaccakkhavasena upaṭṭhānato. (Dīgha Subcommentary, vol. 2, p. 84.)

The causal connection of dependent origination, also called the pattern of conditions (paccayākāra), appears clearly in the continuity of a genuine stream-enterer. The path of stream-entry has dispelled the delusion that conceals knowledge of the causal connection called the truth of origin, so that one has directly realized for oneself: “Whatever is subject to arising is all subject to cessation.” Thus, in that person’s knowledge…



Seeing the causal connection for oneself
Miến / Burmese 8

…that connection is clear. Venerable Ānanda had attained stream-entry. Thus the causal connection of dependent origination appeared clearly in his knowledge.

If a good person thinks they are a stream-enterer, let them examine whether they have directly penetrated the causal connection of dependent origination with the knowledge of right view. Let them ask themselves as follows.

How do the present results—consciousness, mentality-materiality, the six bases, contact, and feeling—arise due to the causes cultivated in the past, namely ignorance, craving, clinging, formations, and kamma? How will the results—consciousness, mentality-materiality, the six bases, contact, and feeling—arise in the future due to ignorance, craving, clinging, formations, and kamma cultivated in the present?

Ask whether you have directly penetrated these matters with the knowledge of right view. If you are still merely feeling your way by a teacher’s words, the answer is already clear. Conclude for yourself that you have yet to become a genuine stream-enterer of the kind the Buddha described. For the noble ones, the Buddha’s true disciples, have directly penetrated the causal connection of dependent origination for themselves with the knowledge of right view.

4. Bahussutabhāva — being learned.

Bahussutānañca catuhatthe ovarake padīpe jalamāne mañcapīṭha…



Learning and the knowledge that distinguishes mentality and materiality
Miến / Burmese 9

…viya nāmarūpaparicchedo pākaṭo hoti. Ayañca āyasmā bahussutānaṃ aggo hoti. Bahussutānubhāvenapissa gambhīropi paccayākāro uttānako viya upaṭṭhāsi. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 83.)

When a lamp is lit in a room four cubits across, the bed and seat in the room can be seen clearly. Venerable Ānanda was foremost among the learned bhikkhus. Through the power of that learning, the causal connection of dependent origination, deep and difficult though it is, appeared plainly to his knowledge as something easy to understand. (Dīgha Commentary, vol. 2, p. 83. Dīgha Subcommentary, vol. 2, p. 84.)

What is a learned person?

Katamañca, bhikkhave, sutadhanaṃ? Idha, bhikkhave, ariyasāvako bahussuto hoti sutadharo sutasannicayo. Ye te dhammā ādikalyāṇā majjhekalyāṇā pariyosānakalyāṇā sātthaṃ sabyañjanaṃ kevalaparipuṇṇaṃ parisuddhaṃ brahmacariyaṃ abhivadanti, tathārūpāssa dhammā bahussutā honti dhātā vacasā paricitā manasānupekkhitā…



The wealth of learning
Miến / Burmese 10

…diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhā. Idaṃ vuccati, bhikkhave, sutadhanaṃ. (Aṅguttara, vol. 2, p. 399.)

Diṭṭhiyā suppaṭividdhāti atthato ca kāraṇato ca paññāya suṭṭhu paṭividdhā, paccakkhaṃ katā. (Aṅguttara Commentary, vol. 2, p. 364.)

Suppaṭividdhāti nijjaṭaṃ niggumbaṃ katvā suṭṭhu yāthāvato paṭividdhā. (Aṅguttara Subcommentary, vol. 2, p. 362.)

“Bhikkhus, what is the wealth of learning?”

“Bhikkhus, here a noble disciple in this dispensation, a disciple of mine who has become noble, has heard much, retains what has been heard, and accumulates it. The disciple has heard many teachings good in the beginning through the practice of virtue, good in the middle through the practice of concentration, and good in the end through the practice of wisdom. Those teachings set forth the holy life complete in meaning and expression, wholly fulfilled and pure: the practice of virtue, concentration, and wisdom in the dispensation, or the practice of the noble eightfold path. The disciple retains these teachings, grows familiar with them through recitation, considers their meaning in the mind, and personally penetrates them directly as they really are with the wisdom of right view, both in meaning and in reason. Bhikkhus, this is called the wealth of learning.” (Aṅguttara, vol. 2, p. 399.)

According to these teachings, a learned good person has both learning received through transmission (āgamasuta) and learning through attainment (adhigamasuta), encompassing what has been heard and what has been directly seen. Such a person already has the knowledge that distinguishes mentality and materiality and the knowledge…



Knowledge that discerns conditions and the three kinds of mundane full understanding
Miến / Burmese 11

…that discerns conditions. A learned person has personally penetrated with the knowledge of right view the material and mental phenomena belonging to the five aggregates subject to clinging, called the truth of suffering, to the point of seeing them as ultimate realities. Such a person has also personally penetrated with the knowledge of right view the causes called the truth of origin. Thus the causal connection of dependent origination appears clearly in that person's continuity.

Therefore, to become a noble person such as a stream-enterer and to pass beyond the suffering of saṃsāra, a good person should strive to penetrate the causal connection of dependent origination directly with the knowledge of right view. One should endeavour to know it clearly through all three kinds of full understanding. Full understanding is of two classes, mundane and supramundane. There are three kinds of mundane and three kinds of supramundane full understanding.

Three kinds of mundane full understanding

1. Full understanding as knowing (ñātapariññā). If a meditator can distinguish and apprehend with knowledge the characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause of each factor of dependent origination—ignorance, formations, consciousness, mentality-materiality, the six bases, contact, feeling, craving, clinging, becoming, birth, and aging and death—that knowledge distinguishes mentality and materiality.



Full understanding as knowing, investigation, and abandoning
Miến / Burmese 12

When one personally penetrates causal connections such as formations arising due to ignorance, consciousness due to formations, and mentality-materiality due to consciousness, that knowledge discerns conditions. The knowledge that distinguishes mentality and materiality and the knowledge that discerns conditions are both called full understanding as knowing (ñātapariññā). They make it possible to know and clearly distinguish the formations that will become the objects of insight knowledge. Hence the name full understanding as knowing.

2. Full understanding as investigation (tīraṇapariññā). When one discerns with wisdom the characteristics of impermanence, suffering, and non-self in the twelve factors of dependent origination, beginning with ignorance and formations, the two knowledges capable of determining these characteristics are comprehension knowledge (sammasanañāṇa) and knowledge of arising and passing away (udayabbayañāṇa). They are called full understanding as investigation. They investigate and determine the factors of dependent origination in terms of impermanence, suffering, and non-self.

3. Full understanding as abandoning (pahānapariññā). When one discerns with wisdom the dissolution of the factors of dependent origination, the insight knowledges beginning with knowledge of dissolution (bhaṅgañāṇa) abandon by momentary opposition the defilements that can be abandoned in that way. At the same time they clearly see the known factors of dependent origination in terms of impermanence, suffering, and non-self. Hence the name full understanding as abandoning. These are the three kinds of mundane full understanding.



Three kinds of supramundane full understanding
Miến / Burmese 13

Three kinds of supramundane full understanding

1. At the culmination of the insight knowledges, that is, the kinds of mundane full understanding, the noble path arises with Nibbāna as its object. The path eradicates the defilements that can be abandoned at that stage and clearly penetrates Nibbāna, the truth to be realized. Thus the noble path is first called full understanding as abandoning (pahānapariññā).

2. By penetration of the object (ārammaṇapaṭivedha), the noble path directly penetrates the truth to be realized, Nibbāna. At the same time, it eradicates the delusion that obscures clear knowledge of the four noble truths. Once this obscuring delusion is eradicated, knowledge of the four truths is accomplished at the same time. This connectedness is called santānānupaccheda here, the non-interruption of the continuum. Because the continuum of mentality and materiality is uninterrupted in this way, a result arises only in the very continuum of mentality and materiality in which its kamma was performed. Remember that kamma already performed cannot simply disappear, and kamma not performed cannot come forward to give a result.

3. The method of non-agency

Avijjāya saṅkhārā mayā uppādetabbā, saṅkhārānaṃ vā viññāṇaṃ amhehīti evamādibyāpārābhāvo abyāpāranayo nāma. Yaṃ sammā passanto kārakassa abhāvāvabodhato attadiṭṭhiṃ pajahati. On the side of the causal phenomena there is no deliberate agency, no arranging for resultant phenomena to arise. On the side of the resultant phenomena there is no deliberate agency either, no arranging that “when the causal phenomena are present, we, the resultant phenomena, shall be present.”

In ignorance there is no deliberate agency, “I shall produce the formations.” In the formations there is no deliberate agency, “We shall produce consciousness.” The absence of deliberate agency in phenomena such as ignorance and formations is called the method of non-agency.

When discerning the meditation subject of dependent origination, a meditator must discern and cultivate it so as to see this method of non-agency correctly. Correct seeing is to see that “phenomena such as ignorance and formations are nirīha, without any endeavor or effort to bring about the result. They are nijjīva, not a living entity capable of making such an effort. They are dhammatā, simply the natural regularity capable of producing the corresponding result”…

Miến / Burmese 104

…One who sees in this way is called sammā passanto, one who sees correctly, one who has right view. Seeing in this way is called seeing correctly.

When the meditator directly sees the method of non-agency correctly with right-view wisdom, the view of self can be abandoned, the belief in a supreme self that acts and creates or a living self that is acted upon and created. This is because the meditator directly sees with right-view wisdom that causal phenomena lack deliberate agency to make resultant phenomena arise.

If the method of non-agency is seen wrongly, one overlooks that although causal phenomena lack deliberate agency to produce their effects, they still exist as causes established by natural regularity and capable of producing effects such as formations. Unable to accept this nature, one may embrace the view of non-action (akiriyadiṭṭhi), that “even if one acts, the action is ineffective.”

Natural regularity (sabhāvaniyama) is the regular nature of arising. In association with a material group, the fire element called fire is oriented upwards, so the flame rises. In association with a material group, air has the nature of distension, so it expands. Modes of occurrence such as these are natural regularity, the nature of arising…

Miến / Burmese 105

When those causes are absent, those effects do not arise. This is called sabhāvaniyamasiddhahetubhāva, the status of being a cause established by the natural regularity of arising. Miến / Burmese 106

4. The method of regularity

Avijjādīhi pana kāraṇehi saṅkhārādīnaṃyeva sambhavo khīrādīhi dadhiādīnaṃ viya, na aññesanti ayaṃ evaṃdhammatānayo nāma. Yaṃ sammā passanto paccayānurūpato phalāvabodhā ahetukadiṭṭhiṃ akiriyadiṭṭhiñca pajahati. Micchā passanto paccayānurūpaṃ phalappavattiṃ aggahetvā yato kutoci yassa kassaci asambhavaggahaṇato ahetukadiṭṭhiñceva niyatavādañca upādiyati. (Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 2, pp. 188–189. Visuddhimagga, vol. 2, pp. 220–221.)

Just as from milk there arise only curds and the like, when results arise from causes such as ignorance, only results such as formations, corresponding to those causes, arise. Other results that do not correspond to the causes do not arise. Put another way, when results arise from the causes ignorance, craving, clinging, formations, and kamma, only effects corresponding to those causes arise, namely consciousness, mentality and materiality, the six bases, contact, and feeling. No other effect that does not correspond to the causes can arise. This is called the method of regularity. Seeing this method correctly, the meditator sees directly with right-view wisdom that results arise corresponding to their causes and can therefore abandon the view of causelessness, which holds that there are no causes, and the view of non-action, which holds that wholesome or unwholesome action is ineffective even when performed. In other words, one can abandon these two wrong views that reject effects arising from causes, thus rejecting both causes and effects.



The doctrine of fatalism
Miến / Burmese 107

A person who sees wrongly may misunderstand the method of regularity. The mistaken way of seeing proceeds as follows. If any effect could arise from any cause, there would be no reason why curds and butter could not arise from water. Yet they cannot arise that way. The person therefore comes arbitrarily to hold that no effect can arise from any cause whatsoever, grasping the doctrine of causelessness.

Seeing wrongly in this way, one fails to grasp that effects arise in accordance with causal phenomena. Unable to grasp or accept that a particular effect arises from a particular cause, one comes to hold firmly to the view of causelessness or to the doctrine of fatalism.

The doctrine of fatalism (niyatavāda) rejects the existence of kamma and its results, holding that the good and bad results experienced by beings are fixed in advance. If asked in turn, “If there are no such causes, on what account do the beings and things before our eyes come into being?”, the proponent of causelessness will answer, “By fate,” meaning that the time at which one will become a human being, a deva, or some kind of animal is predetermined. This teaching is called the doctrine of fatalism. (For details see the doctrine of Makkhali Gosāla in the Sāmaññaphalasutta.) (Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 2, pp. 188–189. Visuddhimagga, vol. 2, pp. 220–221. Mūlaṭīkā, vol. 2, p. 132. Mahāṭīkā, vol. 2, p. 348.)

Therefore a good person should, through right-view wisdom, personally penetrate the nature of dependent origination, the relationship between cause and effect…

Miến / Burmese 108

…and know it directly. Receive this respectfully and remember that such direct knowledge is the best weapon for abandoning and destroying the various wrong doctrines, mistaken teachings, and wrong views.

Attention leading to impulsion and the five aggregates at the first wholesome impulsion
Miến / Burmese 152

…will occur. However, determining within a five-door process does not itself actually decide that the object is desirable, undesirable, permanent, pleasant, self, beautiful, and so forth. Earlier mind-door processes have repeatedly determined the object in such terms as desirable, undesirable, permanent, pleasant, self, and beautiful. When five-door processes then occur, the earlier mind-door processes support them by the power of decisive support. Consequently determining in these later five-door processes occurs in a manner as if determining the object to be desirable, undesirable, permanent, pleasant, self, beautiful, and so forth. That is unwise attention. With that unwise attention as a cause, unwholesome impulsions occur after determining. On the other hand, when determining occurs in the manner of determining the object as a visual object, colour, and so forth, this is wise attention. Wholesome impulsions occur after that determining. Here too, remember that determining is incapable of judging on its own. It occurs as if determining. It is therefore called attention that leads to impulsion, attention to the object as a basic cause of impulsion. (Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 1, p. 177.) The causal discernment of wholesome impulsions that arise from wise attention is presented first.

According to the Pāli texts, commentaries, and subcommentaries above, feeling at eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating, determining, impulsion, and registration arises on account of eye-contact, and is therefore feeling conditioned by eye-contact. According to the Shorter Discourse of Advice to Rāhula (Cūḷarāhulovādasutta), eye-contact gives rise to more than feeling. “Yamidaṃ cakkhusamphassapaccayā uppajjati vedanāgataṃ saññāgataṃ saṅkhāragataṃ viññāṇagataṃ” (Majjhima 3, 326). All four mental aggregates, feeling, perception, formations, and consciousness, arise. Remember, therefore, that eye-contact associated with eye-consciousness supports the arising of each group of four mental aggregates at eye-consciousness, receiving, investigating, determining, impulsion, and registration.

When explaining how eye-contact supports the mental groups that arise on account of it, the commentary describes support within the same process, the eye-door process. The following Pāli passage also occurs.

“Cakkhusamphassapaccayā vedanākkhandho atthi anupādinno anupādāniyo asaṃkiliṭṭho asaṃkilesiko savitakkasavicāro.”

On the basis of such Pāli passages, the commentator explains that the support given by eye-contact to mental groups in other processes cannot be ruled out. This includes the mental groups in a mind-door process that continues to take the visual object. The explanation further states that the commentary describes the support given by eye-contact to the mental groups in the same eye-door process because such support is certain. (Mūlaṭīkā 2, 30.) Distinguish the five aggregates in each mind-moment of wholesome and unwholesome impulsion processes throughout the six classes of visual, sound, odour, taste, tangible, and mental objects. Fully discern the causes and effects by relating them according to the five aggregates. In discerning in this way, bear in mind the following.

Vipassakena pana kammantarañca vipākantarañca ekadesato jānitabbaṃ. (Visuddhimagga 2, 237.)

Following that explanation in the Visuddhimagga, good friend, continue to discern kamma and its results case by case, in their particulars. Resultant consciousnesses during life, such as the five sense consciousnesses, receiving, investigating, and registration in the six kinds of processes beginning with the eye-door process, have different kammic causes. Some resultants during life arise from the very kamma that produces rebirth-linking, life-continuum, and death. Others arise from a different kamma. Look at the twelve kinds of result given by the Buddha. Note that each kind of result has its own corresponding kamma. Therefore, good friend, discern the five past aggregates again in detail. Externally, across successive past and future lives

When you have fully discerned the causal relationship between the five past causes and the present effects in all six classes of objects, as above, apply the same method to discern causes and effects externally. Externally, discern like external phenomena together in general terms.

Proximity causes of the life-continuum
Miến / Burmese 192

Proximity causes of the life-continuum

The consciousness that arises as a factor in the continuity of the stream of consciousness within one life, so that the stream is uninterrupted, is called the life-continuum. It has already been explained that the life-continuum arises from corresponding causes such as ignorance, craving, clinging, formations, kamma, base, object, and contact. Because the life-continuum is the type of consciousness that occurs most frequently during a life, there are also very many proximity causes of it. Briefly, to make the point easy to remember, the mind-moment immediately preceding the life-continuum moment being discerned, the moment that arises with no interval before it, is its proximity cause. Thus the following support by the power of proximity condition.

1. Rebirth-linking supports the first life-continuum moment.

2. The first life-continuum moment supports the second.

3. Each earlier life-continuum moment supports the following one.

4. If the life-continuum occurs after determining, determining supports it.

5. If the life-continuum occurs after impulsion, the final impulsion supports it.

6. If the life-continuum occurs after registration, the second registration supports it.

When one life-continuum moment arises after another, each preceding moment supports the next by the power of proximity condition.

Reaching full understanding as the known (ñātapariññā)

So “idaṃ nāmarūpaṃ na ahetu appaccayā nibbattaṃ, sahetu sappaccayā nibbattaṃ. Ko pana hetu, ko pana paccayo”ti upaparikkhanto “avijjāpaccayā taṇhāpaccayā kammapaccayā āhārapaccayā cā”ti taṃ paccayaṃ vavatthapetvā “atītepi paccayā ceva paccayasamuppannā dhammā ca, anāgatepi etarahipi paccayā ceva paccayasamuppannā dhammā ca, tato uddhaṃ satto vā puggalo vā natthi, suddhasaṅkhārapuñjo evā”ti tīsu addhāsu kaṅkhaṃ vitarati. Ayaṃ pana vipassanāsaṅkhārasallakkhaṇā ñātapariññā nāma. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, 241–242.)

A practitioner who has fully discerned the five aggregates, that is, material and mental phenomena, by bases and doors in all six classes of objects, examines with knowledge as follows. “This mentality and materiality arises when there is a direct cause of its arising, a productive cause, and when there is a sustaining condition, a supporting cause. What, then, is the direct cause of this mentality and materiality? What are its supporting conditions?” In this way the practitioner distinguishes the causes of mentality and materiality. “It arises on account of the cause called ignorance, the cause called craving, the cause called kamma, and the cause called nutriment.” For example, with the arising of ignorance, kamma-produced materiality at rebirth-linking, the materiality aggregate, arises…



Causes and effects in the three periods
Miến / Burmese 194

…Ignorance is the cause. Kamma-produced materiality at rebirth-linking, the materiality aggregate, is the effect. Having distinguished them in this way, the practitioner sees the following.

1. In the past there are only causes and effects.

2. In the future there are only causes and effects.

3. In the present there are only causes and effects.

Beyond these causes and effects there is no person or being who makes or creates, and no person or being made or created. There is no man, woman, person, being, human, deva, Brahmā, living entity, soul, consciousness, or self as an entity. There are simply groups of formations consisting of material phenomena, mental phenomena, causes, and effects. When right-view knowledge penetrates and sees this for oneself, that knowledge can dispel doubt and mistaken speculation about the past, future, and present. It clearly distinguishes the following formations, which are the objects of insight knowledge.

1. Material phenomena.

2. Mental phenomena.

3. Causes.

4. Effects.

The knowledge that clearly distinguishes these formations is called full understanding as the known. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, 241–242.) However, at this point…

…the work is still incomplete. One must make further effort to discern the formations comprising materiality, mentality, causes, and effects by characteristic, function, manifestation, and proximate cause. After making that effort, the next task in the practice is as follows.

Taking root so as to be unable to leave the Dispensation

Evaṃ saṅkhāre sallakkhetvā ṭhito pana bhikkhu no dasabalassa sāsane mūlaṃ otaritvā ṭhito nāma hoti, patiṭṭhaṃ laddhā nāma, cūḷasotāpanno nāma hoti niyatagatikova. Tathārūpaṃ pana utusappāyaṃ puggalasappāyaṃ bhojanasappāyaṃ dhammassavanasappāyaṃ labhitvā ekāsane ekapallaṅkavaragato tīṇi lakkhaṇāni āropetvā vipassanāpaṭipāṭiyā saṅkhāre sammasanto arahattaṃ gaṇhāti. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, 242.)

The practising bhikkhu has clearly distinguished materiality, mentality, causes, and effects as just described. When discerning those formations with knowledge discerning causes and conditions, the bhikkhu reaches purification by overcoming doubt (kaṅkhāvitaraṇavisuddhi), the clear vision that has gone beyond doubt. Thus the bhikkhu has taken root in the Dispensation of the Buddha, the One of Ten Powers, to the point of being unable to leave it. The bhikkhu has gained a footing in the Dispensation and is called a lesser stream-enterer (cūḷasotāpanna), with a secure good destination. Conditions conducive to attaining arahant fruition include the following.

1. Utusappāya, a suitable climate.

2. Puggalasappāya, suitable people such as good spiritual friends, good companions and relations, good teachers, and good fellow students.

3. Bhojanasappāya, suitable food.

4. Dhammassavanasappāya, hearing suitable Dhamma.

Having obtained these four suitable conditions, in a single sitting the practitioner can contemplate formations under the three characteristics, examine them in succession through the course of insight knowledge, and attain arahant fruition. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, 242.)

Discernment in the postures and with clear comprehension

Good friend, you have practised in succession the meditation subjects on serenity, materiality, and mentality before moving on to dependent origination. Thus you already know how to discern the five aggregates, that is, mentality and materiality, using the methods for the postures and for clear comprehension. For practitioners who have discerned the five aggregates, mentality and materiality, the Buddha taught how to continue as follows.

1. Samudayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṃ viharati.

2. Vayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṃ viharati.

3. Samudayavayadhammānupassī vā kāyasmiṃ viharati. (Majjhima 1, 71–72.)

In accordance with these teachings, good friend, see clearly with knowledge the causal relationship of the five aggregates, mentality and materiality, while walking, standing, sitting, or lying down, and during the activities in which clear comprehension is practised. Discern causes and effects as far as you are able.
