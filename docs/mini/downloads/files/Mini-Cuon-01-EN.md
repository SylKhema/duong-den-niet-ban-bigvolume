## Author’s note
Đầu sách / Front matter - 3

In the extensive treatise Nibbānagāminipaṭipadā (The Practice Leading to Nibbāna), the author set out the method for meditators, starting with tranquillity and insight, following the instructions in the texts. To make the method easier to understand, the author cited sources from:

1. The Tipiṭaka.
2. The commentaries.
3. The subcommentaries.

With these textual references and explanations, the treatise became very large. To give meditators a book they could readily carry and read, the author has set out the method again in this abridged edition, reducing quotations from the texts as far as possible. Readers who wish to investigate points of practice that are unclear or doubtful in the light of the texts should consult the larger treatise. Since the textual evidence has been abbreviated in this edition, the author earnestly invites virtuous friends to help supply omissions and correct errors in letters, words or meaning. May all lend their help with an open heart to supply what is lacking…

Pa-Auk Tawya Sayadaw

The Four Noble Truths and the strength of the pāramīs
From the Aparijānanāsutta

“Sabbaṃ, bhikkhave, anabhijānaṃ aparijānaṃ avirājayaṃ appajahaṃ abhabbo dukkhakkhayāya. /pe/ Sabbañca kho, bhikkhave, abhijānaṃ parijānaṃ virājayaṃ pajahaṃ bhabbo dukkhakkhayāya.”

Bhikkhus, without directly knowing and fully understanding all, without becoming dispassionate towards it and abandoning it, one cannot make an end of suffering. /pe/ But, bhikkhus, by directly knowing and fully understanding all, by becoming dispassionate towards it and abandoning it, one can make an end of suffering.

According to the commentary, “abhijānaṃ” refers to full understanding of the known (ñātapariññā), “parijānaṃ” to full understanding by scrutiny (tīraṇapariññā), and “virājayaṃ” and “pajahaṃ” to full understanding by abandoning (pahānapariññā). (Saṃyutta Commentary 3, p. 6, as cited in the original.)

Unless one directly knows and fully understands all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with these three kinds of full understanding, becomes dispassionate towards those aggregates, and abandons craving for them, one cannot attain the end of suffering, Nibbāna. (Saṃyutta 2, pp. 249–250.)

If one directly knows and fully understands all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with the three kinds of full understanding, becomes dispassionate towards them, and abandons craving for them, one can attain the end of suffering, Nibbāna. (Saṃyutta 2, pp. 249–250.)

Therefore, once meditators who aspire to attain Nibbāna, the end of suffering, have understood the meaning of this teaching of the Buddha…

## Four types of people
Miến / Burmese 2

…they must strive to know directly and fully understand all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with the three kinds of full understanding. A good friend who strives in this way and practises the eight factors of the Noble Path that lead to Nibbāna is called one who follows the practice leading to Nibbāna.

Of the eight factors, right view and right intention belong to the wisdom group. Right speech, right action and right livelihood belong to the virtue group. Right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration belong to the concentration group. The training in virtue, concentration and wisdom contained in these eight factors is called the practice that leads out of the round of rebirth (niyyānikapaṭipadā). These are the duties to be fulfilled until Nibbāna is directly realized.

Four types of people

One should also know four types of people who undertake the practice set out in the eight factors:
1. One who understands from a brief statement (Ugghaṭitaññū).
2. One who understands from a detailed explanation (Vipañcitaññū).
3. One who can be guided (Neyya).
4. One for whom the words are the utmost (Padaparama).

There are thus four types of people. (Aṅguttara 1, p. 461, and its Commentary 3, p. 100.)

1. One who understands from a brief statement (Ugghaṭitaññū). Such a person has pāramīs so keen that when the Buddha gives a brief discourse concerning the Four Noble Truths, they can penetrate those truths as soon as the discourse ends. For example, when the Buddha teaches briefly with words such as “cakkhu aniccaṃ…”…


<a id="M01-body-P003"></a>
## Four types of people — continued
Miến / Burmese 3

…“sotaṃ aniccaṃ…”, giving just an outline (mātikā), they can penetrate the Four Noble Truths with path and fruition knowledge as soon as the teaching ends. One whose pāramīs are this keen is called an ugghaṭitaññū.

2. One who understands from a detailed explanation (Vipañcitaññū). The Buddha teaches the Four Noble Truths both briefly and in detail to noble venerable ones such as Sāriputta and Moggallāna. They do not penetrate the truths from a brief statement, but at the end of a detailed explanation they can penetrate the Four Noble Truths with path and fruition knowledge. A person whose pāramīs are sufficiently keen to penetrate the truths in this way is called a vipañcitaññū.

3. One who can be guided (Neyya). Whether the Buddha teaches the Four Noble Truths briefly, in detail, or in various ways, this person does not penetrate them as soon as the teaching ends. ## A point to bear in mind
Miến / Burmese 125

A POINT TO BEAR IN MIND

When a meditator takes one of the foregoing kinds of jhāna, whether access or absorption, as a basis for insight and strives to make it a supporting condition for insight, material clusters begin to appear when he discerns the four great elements and his concentration becomes keen. When he can analyse those material clusters and individually distinguish, with knowledge, the ultimate material phenomena associated with the four great elements in each cluster—their characteristics, functions, manifestations, and proximate causes—he attains Knowledge of Discernment of Materiality (rūpaparicchedañāṇa or rūpapariggahañāṇa). At that point he is said to have personally penetrated genuine ultimate materiality with right view.

Mental phenomena, in turn, occur both as cognitive-process consciousness (vīthicitta) and as process-free consciousness (vīthimuttacitta). The mental phenomena at every mind-moment occur as groups. The groups of consciousness and mental factors present in each such mind-moment are termed non-material clusters or mentality-clusters (arūpakalāpa or nāmakalāpa). (Mahāṭīkā 1, p. 273.) When one discerns with knowledge the ultimate characteristics, functions, manifestations, and proximate causes of the consciousness and mental factors in each mentality-cluster, one attains Knowledge of Discernment of Mentality (nāmaparicchedañāṇa or nāmapariggahañāṇa). One is then said to have personally penetrated genuine ultimate mentality with right view. (Only the consciousness belonging to the eighty-one mundane types is meant here.)

## Insight knowledge must come first
Miến / Burmese 126

If a meditator striving to develop a meditation practice has not yet seen even materiality-clusters and mentality-clusters, or, though seeing them, cannot analyse them, he does not yet know genuine ultimate materiality and mentality. He has not yet attained even Knowledge of Delimitation of Mentality-Materiality.

Again, if he has not looked into the past and future, and knows nothing at all of genuine ultimate past and future mentality-materiality, he has not yet personally penetrated with the eye of wisdom how past causes produce present effects or how present causes produce future effects. In that case he has not yet attained Knowledge of Discerning Conditions (paccayapariggahañāṇa).

The distance between a meditator who has not attained even genuine Knowledge of Delimitation of Mentality-Materiality and genuine Knowledge of Discerning Conditions, and genuine insight knowledge, is as great as that between sky and earth. Since he has not attained even genuine insight knowledge, he is much farther still from the Noble Path knowledge, Noble Fruition knowledge, and Nibbāna that are genuine ultimate Noble Truths. ## Do not mistake falling into the life-continuum for Nibbāna
Miến / Burmese 127

This is a brief statement of the Buddha's reply to the Venerable Susima, who was insisting that Noble Path knowledge taking Nibbāna as object was the outcome of the jhāna attainments. According to this teaching, the Noble Path is the outcome of insight knowledge, and can arise only at its culmination. It is a phenomenon that can arise at the conclusion of genuine insight knowledges when they have afforded support in advance as a strong decisive-support condition (upanissayapaccaya).

Therefore a good person who has not yet attained correct insight knowledge has not yet obtained an entrance to Nibbāna. He stands at a place from which he cannot move, like a post sunk firmly into the ground. (Visuddhimagga 1, p. 124.)

The meditator may describe that fall into the life-continuum by saying, “I no longer know anything,” or, “Both mentality and materiality have ceased.” When this happens, do not conclude that Nibbāna has been realized. It is actually only falling into the life-continuum. Because genuine ultimate materiality and mentality are not yet known, it is not yet even genuine insight knowledge.

Ānāpānassati, bhikkhave, bhāvitā bahulīkatā cattāro satipaṭṭhāne paripūreti. Cattāro satipaṭṭhānā bhāvitā bahulīkatā satta bojjhaṅge paripūrenti. Satta bojjhaṅgā bhāvitā bahulīkatā vijjāvimuttiṃ paripūrenti. (Majjhima 3, 124.)

Bhikkhus, mindfulness of breathing, developed fully through the thirty-two ways and cultivated repeatedly, fulfils the four foundations of mindfulness. The four foundations of mindfulness, developed fully and cultivated repeatedly, fulfil the seven enlightenment factors. The seven enlightenment factors, developed fully and cultivated repeatedly, fulfil knowledge (vijjā), namely the four Noble Path knowledges, and liberation (vimutti), namely the four Noble Fruition knowledges…
