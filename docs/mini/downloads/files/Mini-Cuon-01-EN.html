In the extensive treatise Nibbānagāminipaṭipadā (The Practice Leading to Nibbāna), the author set out the method for meditators, starting with tranquillity and insight, following the instructions in the texts. To make the method easier to understand, the author cited sources from:

1. The Tipiṭaka. 2. The commentaries. 3. The subcommentaries.

With these textual references and explanations, the treatise became very large. To give meditators a book they could readily carry and read, the author has set out the method again in this abridged edition, reducing quotations from the texts as far as possible. Readers who wish to investigate points of practice that are unclear or doubtful in the light of the texts should consult the larger treatise. Since the textual evidence has been abbreviated in this edition, the author earnestly invites virtuous friends to help supply omissions and correct errors in letters, words or meaning. May all lend their help with an open heart to supply what is lacking…

The Four Noble Truths and the strength of the pāramīs From the Aparijānanāsutta

“Sabbaṃ, bhikkhave, anabhijānaṃ aparijānaṃ avirājayaṃ appajahaṃ abhabbo dukkhakkhayāya. /pe/ Sabbañca kho, bhikkhave, abhijānaṃ parijānaṃ virājayaṃ pajahaṃ bhabbo dukkhakkhayāya.”

Bhikkhus, without directly knowing and fully understanding all, without becoming dispassionate towards it and abandoning it, one cannot make an end of suffering. /pe/ But, bhikkhus, by directly knowing and fully understanding all, by becoming dispassionate towards it and abandoning it, one can make an end of suffering.

According to the commentary, “abhijānaṃ” refers to full understanding of the known (ñātapariññā), “parijānaṃ” to full understanding by scrutiny (tīraṇapariññā), and “virājayaṃ” and “pajahaṃ” to full understanding by abandoning (pahānapariññā). (Saṃyutta Commentary 3, p. 6, as cited in the original.)

Unless one directly knows and fully understands all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with these three kinds of full understanding, becomes dispassionate towards those aggregates, and abandons craving for them, one cannot attain the end of suffering, Nibbāna. (Saṃyutta 2, pp. 249–250.)

If one directly knows and fully understands all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with the three kinds of full understanding, becomes dispassionate towards them, and abandons craving for them, one can attain the end of suffering, Nibbāna. (Saṃyutta 2, pp. 249–250.)

Therefore, once meditators who aspire to attain Nibbāna, the end of suffering, have understood the meaning of this teaching of the Buddha…

Four types of people

Miến / Burmese 2

…they must strive to know directly and fully understand all the mental and material phenomena of the five aggregates with the three kinds of full understanding. A good friend who strives in this way and practises the eight factors of the Noble Path that lead to Nibbāna is called one who follows the practice leading to Nibbāna.

Of the eight factors, right view and right intention belong to the wisdom group. Right speech, right action and right livelihood belong to the virtue group. Right effort, right mindfulness and right concentration belong to the concentration group. The training in virtue, concentration and wisdom contained in these eight factors is called the practice that leads out of the round of rebirth (niyyānikapaṭipadā). These are the duties to be fulfilled until Nibbāna is directly realized.

Four types of people

One should also know four types of people who undertake the practice set out in the eight factors: 1. One who understands from a brief statement (Ugghaṭitaññū). 2. One who understands from a detailed explanation (Vipañcitaññū). 3. One who can be guided (Neyya). 4. One for whom the words are the utmost (Padaparama).

There are thus four types of people. (Aṅguttara 1, p. 461, and its Commentary 3, p. 100.)

1. One who understands from a brief statement (Ugghaṭitaññū). Such a person has pāramīs so keen that when the Buddha gives a brief discourse concerning the Four Noble Truths, they can penetrate those truths as soon as the discourse ends. For example, when the Buddha teaches briefly with words such as “cakkhu aniccaṃ…”…

Four types of people — continued

Miến / Burmese 3

…“sotaṃ aniccaṃ…”, giving just an outline (mātikā), they can penetrate the Four Noble Truths with path and fruition knowledge as soon as the teaching ends. One whose pāramīs are this keen is called an ugghaṭitaññū.

2. One who understands from a detailed explanation (Vipañcitaññū). The Buddha teaches the Four Noble Truths both briefly and in detail to noble venerable ones such as Sāriputta and Moggallāna. They do not penetrate the truths from a brief statement, but at the end of a detailed explanation they can penetrate the Four Noble Truths with path and fruition knowledge. A person whose pāramīs are sufficiently keen to penetrate the truths in this way is called a vipañcitaññū.

3. One who can be guided (Neyya). Whether the Buddha teaches the Four Noble Truths briefly, in detail, or in various ways, this person does not penetrate them as soon as the teaching ends. Afterwards, however, relying on wise friends and diligently practising virtue, concentration and wisdom according to the eight path factors, they can gradually penetrate the Four Noble Truths and attain path and fruition knowledge. Such a person is called a neyya.

4. One for whom the words are the utmost (Padaparama). Whether taught the Four Noble Truths briefly, in detail, or in various ways, this person does not penetrate them. Even if, after hearing the Dhamma, they strive correctly to practise the eight path factors under the guidance of wise people, they cannot penetrate the Four Noble Truths in that very life. Such a person is called a padaparama.

The four types of people and the seven qualities of a good person

Miến / Burmese 4

Although they cannot yet penetrate the Four Noble Truths, by rightly practising the eight path factors they can develop wholesome qualities that constitute pāramīs. They will penetrate the Noble Truths in a future life.

Of the four types, the ugghaṭitaññū and vipañcitaññū can penetrate the Four Noble Truths with path and fruition knowledge upon hearing the Buddha teach them, the former from a brief statement and the latter from a detailed explanation. For the neyya and padaparama, hearing about the practice of the eight path factors is insufficient. They must undertake that practice correctly, in accordance with the Dhamma. When their pāramīs are mature, by practising in this way they can penetrate the truths in this very life. Such people are neyya. If their pāramīs are not yet mature enough for them to penetrate the truths in this life, they are padaparama. The author is presenting the practice of the eight path factors chiefly for these two types. Neyya means someone who can be guided. The present age is the age of people who can be guided in this way. Practice is therefore especially necessary for them. Let us now consider the seven qualities of a good person (sappurisadhamma). (Dīgha 3, p. 223, and Subcommentary 2, p. 50.)

Seven qualities of a good person

1. Saddhā — Faith. It is confidence that the Buddha is an Arahant, fully enlightened, and that this Arahant is indeed the Buddha. In particular, it is firm confidence that an Arahant has eradicated all defilements without remainder…

Chỉ dẫn số tập/trang cuối đoạn cần kiểm lại ở hình phóng lớn; giữ là dẫn theo nguồn, chưa tra độc lập.

Seven qualities of a good person — continued

Miến / Burmese 5

…and is utterly pure. This is the characteristic of faith. In particular, one has wholehearted confidence that the Buddha is an Arahant who has eradicated all defilements and is utterly pure.

2. Pariyatti — Learning. One is learned in the Buddha’s words, the teachings he proclaimed. In the dispensations of past Buddhas one also strove to become accomplished in this learning.

3. Sākacchā — Discussion. One discusses and asks and answers questions with good friends, respectfully and with a desire to understand, without disdain or contempt. In particular, one also strove to develop this quality in the dispensations of past Buddhas.

4. Paripucchā — Inquiry. Concerning profound teachings on the aggregates, bases, elements, Noble Truths and dependent origination, one learns the intended meaning (attha) and the meaning of the words (pada), and asks how passages follow and relate to one another (anusandhi). In other words, one studies the explanations. In the dispensations of past Buddhas one also made a special effort in this regard to understand profound teachings such as the aggregates. (Aṅguttara Commentary 2, p. 272.)

5. Pubbayoga — “Pubbayogo nāma pubbayojanaṃ sāsane gatapaccāgatikabhāvena yāva anulomaṃ gotrabhusamīpaṃ tāva vipassanānuyogo.”

Pubbayoga is former endeavour. Within the Dispensation, one diligently practises insight while carrying the meditation subject out and back (gatapaccāgatika), as far as conformity, close to change-of-lineage. (Visuddhimagga 1, p. 72.)

“Yāva anulomagotrabhusamīpaṃ saṅkhārupekkhāñāṇaṃ.”

As far as knowledge of equanimity about formations, close to conformity and change-of-lineage. (Mahāṭīkā 1, p. 104.)

Former endeavour and former cultivation

Miến / Burmese 6

Former endeavour means having diligently practised a meditation subject in the dispensations of past Buddhas. One took the meditation subject along when going to the village for alms and brought it back when returning to the monastery. One diligently practised tranquillity and insight, advancing towards knowledge of equanimity about formations, a stage of insight leading to emergence close to the Noble Path. This is called pubbayoga. (Visuddhimagga 1, p. 72, and Mahāṭīkā 1, p. 104.)

In the commentary on the causes of sharp knowledge in scrutiny, however, this former endeavour (pubbayoga) is called former cultivation (pubbavāsa). Faith, learning, discussion and inquiry are the same. Pubbavāsa is explained as follows:

“Pubbavāso nāma pubbayogāvacaratā atītabhave haraṇapaccāharaṇanayena parihaṭakammaṭṭhānatā.” (Source cited in the original: Aṅguttara, Commentary, 2, p. 272.)

In past lives, one took the meditation subject along when leaving the monastery for alms in the village and diligently practised while going. One brought the subject back when returning to the monastery and diligently practised on the way back. Having carried, maintained and discerned with knowledge a subject of tranquillity and insight meditation in this way is called pubbavāsa. (Aṅguttara Commentary 2, p. 272.)

Here, the author calls past practice of pubbayoga pubbavāsa, without distinguishing between the dispensations of earlier Buddhas and that of the present Buddha. The author also mentions phenomena investigated by insight, such as the truth of suffering, under “saccādīsu” (Aṅguttara Commentary 2, p. 272).

Former endeavour — continued

Miến / Burmese 7

The reference here extends beyond knowledge of equanimity about formations in this life. Whether in the dispensations of past Buddhas or in the present Buddha’s Dispensation, past insight practice is called former endeavour (pubbayoga). It investigates, as impermanent, suffering and non-self, formations of two kinds: the five aggregates subject to clinging, which constitute the truth of suffering, and ignorance, craving, clinging, formations, kamma and so forth, which belong to the truth of the origin of suffering.

“Etesu pana kāraṇesu pariyatti, savanaṃ, paripucchāti imāni tīṇi pabhedasseva balavakāraṇāni. Pubbayogo hi adhigamassa balavapaccayo, pabhedassa hoti na hotīti? Na pana tathā. Pariyattisavanaparipucchā hi pubbe hontu vā mā vā, pubbayogena pubbe ceva etarahi ca saṅkhārasammasanaṃ vinā paṭisambhidā nāma natthi. Ime pana dvepi ekato hutvā paṭisambhidā upatthambhetvā visadā karonti.” (Source cited in the original: Abhidhamma Commentary, vol. 2, p. 373.)

Of the five causes just discussed, learning (pariyatti), listening (savana) and inquiry (paripucchā) are strong causes specifically for differences in the degree of analytical knowledge (paṭisambhidāñāṇa). Former endeavour (pubbayoga) is a strong cause for attainment (adhigama), namely the Noble Paths and Fruitions. Does that mean former endeavour does not also account for differences in analytical knowledge? It does play a part. Yet although former endeavour is a strong cause for attainment of the Noble Paths and Fruitions…

Strong and supporting causes of analytical knowledge

Miến / Burmese 8

…it is not a strong cause for differences in the degree of analytical knowledge. It is nevertheless a supporting cause (upatthambhaka).

Learning (pariyatti), listening (savana) and inquiry (paripucchā) may or may not have taken place in the dispensations of past Buddhas or in earlier lives during the present Buddha’s Dispensation. As for former endeavour (pubbayoga), without insight practice in past lives under previous Buddhas or in the present life, analytical knowledge cannot be attained. This practice consists in scrutinizing formations belonging to the truths of suffering and its origin as impermanent, suffering and non-self.

Former endeavour (pubbayoga) and attainment (adhigama) together support the arising of analytical knowledge and make it clear. Thus, though former endeavour is not the strong cause of differences in degree, it supports and sustains analytical knowledge. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, pp. 372–373, and Mūlaṭīkā 2, p. 193.)

The four analytical knowledges (Paṭisambhidāñāṇa)

Miến / Burmese 9

A question arises here: do all noble disciples attain analytical knowledge? The Sammohavinodanī commentary explains:

“Ariyasāvako no paṭisambhidāpatto nāma natthi.” (Abhidhamma Commentary, tập 2, p. 372.)

There is no noble disciple who has not attained analytical knowledge. (Abhidhamma Commentary 2, p. 372.)

When a meditator takes one of the foregoing kinds of jhāna, whether access or absorption, as a basis for insight and strives to make it a supporting condition for insight, material clusters begin to appear when he discerns the four great elements and his concentration becomes keen. At that point he is said to have personally penetrated genuine ultimate materiality with right view.

Mental phenomena, in turn, occur both as cognitive-process consciousness (vīthicitta) and as process-free consciousness (vīthimuttacitta). The mental phenomena at every mind-moment occur as groups. The groups of consciousness and mental factors present in each such mind-moment are termed non-material clusters or mentality-clusters (arūpakalāpa or nāmakalāpa). (Mahāṭīkā 1, p. 273.) When one discerns with knowledge the ultimate characteristics, functions, manifestations, and proximate causes of the consciousness and mental factors in each mentality-cluster, one attains Knowledge of Discernment of Mentality (nāmaparicchedañāṇa or nāmapariggahañāṇa).
